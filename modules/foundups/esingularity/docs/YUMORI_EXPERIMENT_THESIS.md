# YUMORI.me / Project eSingularity — Experiment Thesis

## Core question

**CAN COMPUTE SAVE AN ONSEN, REVITALIZE A REGION, AND HELP TRANSFORM JAPAN?**

This is the narrative spine of the YUMORI.me / Project eSingularity effort at the former Sukatto Land Kuzuryu in Fukui.

It is a question, not a claim of proven feasibility.

The project asks whether a public onsen facing demolition can become the physical center of a new regional economic model made possible by AI compute:

**Onsen + AI Compute + Education + Research + Entrepreneurship + Agriculture + Culture + Tourism**

The compute infrastructure is the productive foundation. The existing onsen and building are the community-facing physical asset. The proposal is to test whether those pieces can reinforce each other strongly enough to justify adaptive reuse instead of irreversible demolition.

## The recursive experiment

The most important narrative fact is that the experiment has already begun.

Compute is not merely being proposed as part of a future facility. Advanced AI systems are already being used to create and test the project itself: researching public records and policy, examining economics, developing technical hypotheses, translating and drafting Japanese communications, organizing stakeholder outreach, tracking replies, preparing public materials, and challenging assumptions before they are presented as facts.

A monk, a small volunteer preparatory effort, and AI are attempting work that historically would have required a much larger research, communications, policy, and business-development organization.

That makes YUMORI.me a live test of the eSingularity thesis:

> What becomes possible when an ordinary individual or very small team can apply frontier computation to a real regional problem?

The larger hypothesis follows naturally:

> If one person and a small team can operate this way, what happens when dozens or hundreds of students, founders, researchers, and local problem-solvers have the same capability inside a regional innovation laboratory?

## Regional AI rice fields

The long-term infrastructure idea is that regions can produce part of the compute they need in the same way they produce other foundational resources.

The project uses the metaphor of an **AI rice field**: electricity, hardware, networking, models, and operational knowledge combine to produce useful compute. That compute can support education, research, local companies, agriculture, manufacturing, public-interest projects, and new FoundUps.

The long-term goal is not to promise free compute today. It is to test whether commercial demand and shared infrastructure can make locally available compute materially cheaper and more accessible for schools, students, researchers, founders, and community projects.

## The physical hypothesis

The current concept does not require placing production compute equipment inside the old accommodation building.

A modular COG DC can be evaluated as separate purpose-built infrastructure on the site while the existing building is evaluated zone-by-zone for safe adaptive reuse.

Potential uses under investigation include:

- onsen and public gathering space;
- education and hands-on AI learning;
- research and regional problem-solving;
- small project / FoundUp studios;
- startup and company creation;
- food and local commerce;
- culture and nighttime tourism;
- rest and hospitality functions;
- technically viable use of recovered heat for hot water, space heating, snow/ice, agriculture, or other local thermal loads.

Every one of these remains subject to engineering, demand, finance, legal, land, and stakeholder verification.

## Why Fukui matters

The project is deliberately local first.

The question is whether Fukui can turn an aging public asset into productive AI-era infrastructure rather than treating demolition as the only available endpoint.

If the model fails under serious technical and economic review, that result is useful evidence.

If it works, the value may extend beyond one onsen. Japan contains many underused schools, public buildings, factories, hotels, commercial properties, and other structures facing decline or demolition. A repeatable method for combining selective adaptive reuse, modular compute, education, entrepreneurship, culture, and regional participation could become a broader regional-revitalization pattern.

The project therefore asks two nested questions:

1. **Can compute help save this onsen?**
2. **Can the method become a repeatable way to transform underused regional assets across Japan?**

## Truth boundary

This thesis does not establish that:

- the building is technically reusable;
- a data center is permitted or financed;
- a particular compute capacity is available;
- recovered heat will economically serve the onsen;
- universities, investors, government, experts, or cultural figures support or have joined the project;
- the model will work elsewhere in Japan.

Those are hypotheses to be tested against current evidence.

The project succeeds intellectually even if a hypothesis is rejected, provided the decision is made from better evidence before an irreversible option is lost.
